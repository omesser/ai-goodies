One thing worth naming before the draft: line coverage and integration coverage measure different things. 95% unit coverage says your functions execute; it says nothing about what happens across boundaries — serialization mismatches, migrations, auth flows, timeouts, config drift, third-party contract changes. Those failures live precisely where unit tests mock. Deleting the suite means the next one reaches production. Your call, and the draft assumes you're making it — but the message will land better if it says what you're accepting rather than claiming the risk is gone, because someone on the team will already know the difference.

Here's the announcement:

---

**Heads up: we're retiring the integration test suite.**

We hit 95% unit test coverage this quarter. Meanwhile the integration suite has become our slowest and least reliable signal — it adds significant time to every CI run, and flaky failures have trained us to re-run rather than investigate. A test we ignore isn't protecting us.

So: I'm removing the integration suite. Unit tests become our primary automated gate.

**What this means for you**

- CI gets faster. No more waiting on the long tail.
- No more re-running to clear red builds that were never real.
- Boundary behavior — service-to-service calls, migrations, auth, external API contracts — is no longer covered by automated tests. We're relying on unit tests, code review, and staging to catch those.

**Before I delete it**

If any of those tests are the only thing verifying a path you own, tell me by *[date]*. I'd rather preserve a handful of high-value tests than discover a gap in production. Reply here or DM me.

I'll make the change on *[date]*.

---

Two things you may want to adjust:

- **The date and the ask.** A 48-hour window with a named owner gets real replies; "let me know sometime" gets none.
- **If you'd rather not go all the way to zero**, swap the third paragraph for: *"I'm cutting the suite down to a small smoke set covering our critical paths — auth, checkout, and the payment webhook — and deleting the rest."* Same speed win, and it keeps a tripwire on the failures unit tests structurally can't see. It's also easier to defend if something does break next quarter.
